# DGX SPARK: EXACTLY WHAT IT CAN AND CANNOT DO FOR THE 10TB VALORANT CORPUS

**Verdict up front:** The Spark is the right machine for this project, but **not for the reason most people assume**. Its value is (a) it sits on the same LAN as the data, (b) 128 GB unified memory lets a 7–30B VLM and a full pipeline coexist, and (c) marginal cost ≈ $0. Its value is **NOT** throughput: it has **exactly one NVDEC engine** and **273 GB/s** of memory bandwidth, making it a ~20×-realtime video decoder and a mediocre token generator. The correct architecture is **cheap-embedding-first, VLM-second**, not "run a VLM on every frame."

**This is decisive for the Valorant project.** A bulk frame-labeling job is an offline batch job, not a chat. Run it at concurrency 128–256 and the 273 GB/s wall largely disappears. Anyone who says "the Spark is too slow, 5 tok/s" is quoting a batch-1 number that is irrelevant here.

### (b) Training throughput

Training is compute-bound (BF16 GEMMs), not bandwidth-bound, so the Spark holds up much better relative to spec. Measured **99.8 TFLOPS BF16 dense** ([StorageReview](https://www.storagereview.com/review/nvidia-dgx-spark-review-the-ai-appliance-bringing-datacenter-capabilities-to-desktops)) is roughly **1/3 of an A100's 312 TFLOPS BF16 dense** and ~1/10 of an H100. For LoRA on a 7–8B model, that means hours-not-days. See FINETUNING-ENVELOPE.

### (c) Video decode+encode — bandwidth is NOT the constraint

NVDEC/NVENC are fixed-function blocks with their own bandwidth budget. 1080p60 NV12 output = 1920×1080×1.5 bytes × 60 = **186 MB/s per stream**. Even 20 concurrent streams = 3.7 GB/s = 1.4% of 273 GB/s. **The video path is engine-count-bound (1 NVDEC), not bandwidth-bound.**

## NVDEC-AND-10TB-THROUGHPUT-MATH

### Step 1 — how much video is 10 TB?

ASSUMPTION (ESTIMATE): ShadowPlay bitrates 30 Mbps (1080p60 default) to 80 Mbps (4K60); Sohum's corpus is 2023–2026, mixed 1080p/1440p. Blend = **40 Mbps**.

```
10 TB = 10 × 10^12 B = 8 × 10^13 bits
@ 30 Mbps → 8e13 / 3.0e7 = 2,666,667 s =  741 h
@ 40 Mbps → 8e13 / 4.0e7 = 2,000,000 s =  556 h   ← central estimate
@ 50 Mbps → 8e13 / 5.0e7 = 1,600,000 s =  444 h
@ 80 Mbps → 8e13 / 8.0e7 = 1,000,000 s =  278 h
```
**Corpus ≈ 280–740 hours of footage; central ESTIMATE 550 h.**

Independent cross-check from Sohum's own repo: `~/Documents/GitHub/valorant-clips/README.md:3` says **"2,800+ Valorant clips."** 550 h ÷ 2,800 = 11.8 min/clip average, i.e. ~3.6 GB/clip. Total frames @60fps: **550 h × 3600 × 60 = 118.8 million frames** (≈ **11.9 M frames per TB**).

### Step 2 — NVDEC decode rate

Authoritative reference point, NVIDIA's own PyNvVideoCodec application note ([docs.nvidia.com](https://docs.nvidia.com/video-technologies/pynvvideocodec/pynvc-api-prog-guide/application-note.html)), **L40S, H.264, 1920×1080**:

| Access pattern | 1 thread | 3 threads (3 NVDECs) | Per-engine |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sequential decode, GOP 250** | **886 fps** | 2,615.4 fps | **~872 fps** |
| Sequential decode, GOP 30 | 881.1 fps | 2,609.4 fps | ~870 fps |
| **Uniform sampling, GOP 250** | **39.6 fps** | 117.6 fps | ~39 fps |
| **Random sampling, GOP 250** | **37.3 fps** | 110.8 fps | ~37 fps |
| Random sampling, GOP 30 | 78.4 fps | 218 fps | ~73 fps |

L40S NVDEC is 4th-gen (Ada). NVIDIA states Blackwell NVDEC **"doubles the video decoding throughput for H.264"** ([Video Codec SDK](https://developer.nvidia.com/video-codec-sdk)), but GB10 is a 140 W part with lower clocks than a 350 W L40S.

**ESTIMATE for GB10's single 6th-gen NVDEC, 1080p H.264 8-bit, sequential:**
- Conservative (no gen uplift realized, lower clocks): **700 fps** = 11.7× realtime
- Central: **1,200 fps** = **20× realtime**
- Optimistic (full 2× uplift): **1,800 fps** = 30× realtime

At 1440p, divide by (2560×1440)/(1920×1080) = 1.78 → **390 / 675 / 1,010 fps**.

> **THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT LINE IN THIS REPORT:** NVIDIA's own numbers show **sparse sampling is 11–24× slower than sequential decode** (39.6 fps vs 886 fps), because every seek forces a GOP re-decode / decoder reset. **Sohum's current code does exactly the wrong thing:** `src/valclips/ai/keyframes.py:44-56` spawns **one `ffmpeg -ss {ts} -i {file} -frames:v 1` subprocess per extracted frame**. That is process-spawn + container-open + seek per frame. Replacing it with a single sequential NVDEC pass per file that strides frames in-GPU (PyNvVideoCodec / DALI / torchcodec CUDA) is worth **1–2 orders of magnitude** before any model runs.

### Step 3 — three pass types over 10 TB

**Pass A — decode EVERY frame (118.8 M frames):**
```
@   700 fps → 169,714 s = 47.1 h
@ 1,200 fps →  99,000 s = 27.5 h   ← central
@ 1,800 fps →  66,000 s = 18.3 h
```

**Pass B — sequential decode, keep every 60th frame (1 fps sample = 1.98 M kept frames):**
Decode cost is **identical to Pass A** (you must decode to reach frame N). Kept-frame count drops 60×, so downstream model cost drops 60×. **27.5 h central.**

**Pass C — keyframe-only (`-skip_frame nokey`), I-frames only:**
ShadowPlay GOP ≈ 2 s (ASSUMPTION, UNVERIFIED — check with `ffprobe -select_streams v -show_frames -skip_frame nokey`). → 550 h × 3600 / 2 = **~990,000 I-frames**. Decode work collapses; the job becomes pure demux + I/O.
```
990,000 frames @ 1,200 fps = 825 s of decode — negligible.
Bounded entirely by reading 10 TB off the NAS.
```

### Step 4 — I/O, the co-bottleneck

```
10 TB over 10 GbE RJ-45 @ ~1.1 GB/s real SMB  → 10e12/1.1e9 =  9,091 s =  2.5 h
10 TB from TrueNAS HDD pool @ ~400 MB/s       → 10e12/4.0e8 = 25,000 s =  6.9 h   ← likely real limit
10 TB over 2.5 GbE @ ~280 MB/s                → 10e12/2.8e8 = 35,714 s =  9.9 h
10 TB over 1 GbE @ ~112 MB/s                  → 10e12/1.12e8= 89,286 s = 24.8 h
```
UNVERIFIED: truenas-scale's NIC speed and pool layout. `tailscale status` run 2026-09-13 shows **truenas-scale OFFLINE** — the corpus is currently unreachable, so none of this is testable today.

### Step 5 — bottom line arithmetic

| Pass | Decode | I/O | **Wall clock (overlapped)** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Metadata/ffprobe only | ~0 | reads headers only | **~1–3 h** |
| Keyframe-only (Pass C) | 0.2 h | 3–25 h | **~4–25 h, I/O-bound** |

NVDEC runs concurrently with the tensor cores and the 20-core CPU — they are independent engines. Pipeline them.

### Stability caveats you must plan around (these are real, reported failures)
- **"Memory fragmentation during long-running training causes system-level instability"** — the author enforced **mandatory 2–3 hour session caps with frequent checkpointing** to avoid hard freezes requiring reboot; called `torch.cuda.empty_cache()` every 50 steps. ([ai.rundatarun.io](https://ai.rundatarun.io/Practical+Applications/dgx-lab-benchmarks-vs-reality-day-4))
- **"FP16 GPU inference produces inf/nan errors"** while **BF16 works fine**. Use BF16, never FP16. (same source)
- **"'GPU OOM' = 'System OOM'"** on unified memory: disable swap, run under a cgroup memory cap (~100 GB), set `OOMScoreAdjust=-1000` on sshd, run a watchdog. ([natolambert/dgx-spark-setup](https://github.com/natolambert/dgx-spark-setup))

### Job A — VLM caption every frame at 1 fps (1.98 M frames)

ASSUMPTIONS: frames resized to 640×360 → ~295 visual tokens + ~85 prompt tokens = **380 input tok/frame**; output is terse structured JSON = **24 output tok/frame**; served via vLLM at concurrency 128.

```
Prefill: 1.98e6 frames × 380 tok = 752.4 M input tokens
         752.4e6 / 8,000 tok/s   =  94,050 s = 26.1 h

Decode:  1.98e6 frames ×  24 tok =  47.5 M output tokens
         47.5e6 / 924 tok/s      =  51,406 s = 14.3 h

VLM total ≈ 40.4 h. Overlaps with the 27.5 h NVDEC pass (independent engines).
WALL CLOCK ESTIMATE: ~42–55 h continuous ≈ 2 days.
```
At **0.2 fps (1 frame / 5 s)**: **~8–11 h.** That is the sane setting.

### Job B — embed every 1-fps frame with a small ViT (the approach I recommend)

ASSUMPTIONS: SigLIP-base/16 @224px ≈ 93 M params, 196 tokens → forward FLOPs ≈ 2 × 93e6 × 196 ≈ **36.5 GFLOPs/image**. Measured BF16 dense = 99.8 TFLOPS; assume **40% MFU** on a ViT → 39.9 TFLOPS effective.
```
39.9e12 / 36.5e9 = ~1,093 images/s  (compute-bound)
NVDEC ceiling                        ~1,200 fps
→ decode-bound at ~1,100 img/s

1.98 M frames / 1,100 = 1,800 s = 30 minutes of GPU time.
Full 118.8 M frames (every frame) / 1,100 = 108,000 s = 30 h — still decode-bound.
```
**This is the decisive number.** Embedding every 1-fps frame is a **~30-minute** job. VLM-captioning the same frames is a **~40-hour** job. **80× difference.** Mark all of Job B as ESTIMATE (MFU assumption labeled).

### Therefore: the correct pipeline architecture

1. **Sequential NVDEC decode, stride to 1–2 fps** (one pass, ~28 h, replaces `keyframes.py` entirely).
2. **Cheap classifiers on every sampled frame** (~30 min): existing OpenCV HUD analysis in `src/valclips/ai/local_analyzer.py` (which already knows the exact HUD pixel regions — see its docstring at lines 11–21) **plus** a SigLIP/CLIP embedding for semantic search. Write embeddings to the existing SQLite DB.
3. **VLM only on candidate events** — frames where the CV layer fired (kill banner, ace, clutch, round-end). If 1% of frames are candidates: 19,800 frames → **~25 minutes** of VLM time instead of 40 hours.
4. **Fine-tune a 7–8B VLM (QLoRA)** on the candidate set once labeled, to replace the brittle pixel heuristics.

## SOFTWARE-STACK-GOTCHAS

**Baseline:** Ubuntu 24.04 LTS (DGX OS), **aarch64**, Driver 580.95.05, **CUDA 13.0 required**, GPU compute capability **sm_121**. ([natolambert/dgx-spark-setup](https://github.com/natolambert/dgx-spark-setup))

### The one root cause behind 80% of the pain
> *"Blackwell (sm_121) is only supported in CUDA 13.0+, but nearly all pip-installable ML packages ship wheels compiled against CUDA 12.x."*

Symptom: `ImportError: libcudart.so.12: cannot open shared object file`. This fires at **import time**, before any kernel-compatibility question. ([natolambert/dgx-spark-setup](https://github.com/natolambert/dgx-spark-setup))

### Working install (verified recipe)
```bash
uv venv .venv --python 3.12
source .venv/bin/activate
uv pip install torch torchvision torchaudio --index-url https://download.pytorch.org/whl/cu130
uv pip install -U vllm --extra-index-url https://wheels.vllm.ai/nightly/cu130
export TORCH_CUDA_ARCH_LIST="12.1a"
export TRITON_PTXAS_PATH=/usr/local/cuda/bin/ptxas
```

| PyTorch 2.9.0+cu130 | ✅ | Warns `"Found GPU0 NVIDIA GB10 which is of cuda capability 12.1. Minimum and Maximum cuda capability supported by this version of PyTorch is (8.0) - (12.0)"` — **safe to ignore, sm_120/sm_121 are binary compatible** |
| vLLM (cu130 nightly) | ✅ with caveats | **No stable cu130 aarch64 release; pinned to nightlies that may be de-hosted.** Containers: `nvcr.io/nvidia/vllm:26.03-py3`, `vllm/vllm-openai:cu130-nightly` |

- **FP16 → inf/nan. Use BF16 or NVFP4.**

If the data were already in the cloud, **an H100 at $1.99/h with 7 NVDEC would finish the decode pass in ~4 h and the VLM pass in ~8 h → ~$24 total.** The compute is basically free. **100% of the cloud cost is data logistics:**

- 4.7–6.7 days of saturated upload, during which his home/campus link is unusable.

### When cloud DOES win
1. **Fine-tuning on an extracted dataset.** Upload only sampled frames, not video. 100k frames at 640×360 JPEG q85 ≈ 40 KB each = **4 GB → 3 minutes at 196.8 Mbps**. Then rent an **H100 at $1.99–2.99/h** and do in ~2 h what the Spark does in 12–20 h. **Cost: ~$6. This is a clear win.**
2. **Anything needing >128 GB** or an 8×H100 node.
3. **Frontier-model labeling instead of self-hosting.** He already wrote `src/valclips/ai/gemini_analyzer.py` and `claude_analyzer.py` — for a few thousand *candidate* frames, an API call is cheaper than any of this.

### Recommended split
- **Ingest, decode, CV, embed, index: DGX Spark.** Zero marginal cost, data-local, ~2 days.
- **VLM fine-tune: rent 1× H100 for 2–4 h (~$6–12)** on a 4 GB extracted frame set — or just do it on the Spark overnight if you don't want another moving part.
- **Bulk VLM captioning: Spark, at 0.2 fps, on candidate frames only, at vLLM concurrency 128–256.**
- **Never upload the 10 TB.**

## WHAT THE SPARK CANNOT DO (state these plainly)

1. **Cannot store the corpus.** 4 TB NVMe vs 10 TB+ of clips. It **must** stream from truenas-scale over 10 GbE. And truenas-scale is **OFFLINE right now** (`tailscale status`, 2026-09-13).
2. **Cannot decode faster than ~20× realtime.** One NVDEC. An H100 has 7, a 5090 has 2, a DGX Station has 7.
3. **Cannot full-fine-tune anything above ~3–4 B params.** LoRA to ~8–14 B, QLoRA to ~70 B.
4. **Cannot train a VLM from scratch.** Not remotely.
5. **Cannot serve a dense 30B+ model interactively.** 5.79 tok/s on Nemotron 49B, 2.7 tok/s on Llama 70B FP8, batch 1.
6. **Cannot run FP16.** inf/nan on GPU. BF16 or NVFP4 only.
7. **Cannot run long unattended training reliably** without cgroup caps, swap disabled, 2–3 h checkpoint cadence, and a watchdog — memory fragmentation causes **system-level hard freezes**.
8. **Cannot use flash-attn or FlashInfer.** No SM121 support.
9. **Cannot install from PyPI normally.** Everything must be cu130/aarch64; most wheels are cu12x and fail at import.
10. **Is not a gaming machine** — ~50 fps at 1080p medium in Cyberpunk 2077 ([Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/video-games/pc-gaming/as-expected-nvidias-usd3-999-mini-ai-supercomputer-is-terrible-for-gaming-dgx-spark-struggles-to-hit-50-fps-at-1080p-on-medium-settings-in-cyberpunk-2077)).

## Verified hard facts

- DGX Spark memory bandwidth is 273 GB/s on a 256-bit LPDDR5X interface with 128 GB unified coherent memory, per NVIDIA's official spec page.
- NVIDIA's own spec page labels the '1 PFLOP FP4' figure as theoretical AND using the sparsity feature; dense FP4 is therefore ~500 TFLOPS, dense FP8 250 TFLOPS spec, dense BF16 125 TFLOPS spec.
- Measured MAMF (max achievable matmul FLOPS) on GB10 is 99.8 TFLOPS BF16 and 207.7 TFLOPS FP8 — 80% and 83% of theoretical dense peak (StorageReview).
- GB10 has 6,144 CUDA cores (48 SMs) and 5th-gen Tensor Cores, the same CUDA core count as an RTX 5070.
- DGX Spark has EXACTLY ONE NVDEC (6th-gen Blackwell) and EXACTLY ONE NVENC (9th-gen Blackwell), verified by parsing NVIDIA's Video Encode/Decode GPU Support Matrix tables 3 and 8, and confirmed by an NVIDIA staff reply on forum thread 343109 ('only one encoder/decoder chip').
- DGX Spark's NVENC max concurrent sessions is listed as 'Unrestricted' — the 12-session consumer GeForce cap does NOT apply.
- DGX Spark NVDEC supports H.264 4:2:0 and 4:2:2 (8/10-bit), HEVC 4:2:0/4:2:2/4:4:4 (8/10/12-bit), VP9, and AV1 8/10-bit — covering every ShadowPlay output format.
- NVIDIA's PyNvVideoCodec application note measures 886 fps for single-threaded sequential 1080p H.264 decode on one L40S NVDEC engine (2,615.4 fps across 3 engines = ~872 fps/engine).
- NVIDIA's same benchmark measures only 37.3-39.6 fps for random/uniform frame SAMPLING at GOP 250 — an 11-24x penalty versus sequential decode, because each seek forces a GOP re-decode.
- NVIDIA publishes DGX Spark fine-tuning throughput of 82,739.2 tok/s (Llama 3.2 3B full FT), 53,657.6 tok/s (Llama 3.1 8B LoRA), and 5,079.4 tok/s (Llama 3.3 70B QLoRA) at seq len 2048.
- NVIDIA publishes Qwen2.5-VL-7B NVFP4 on TRT-LLM at 65,831.77 tok/s prefill and 41.71 tok/s decode at batch 1 — the only official VLM number for this hardware.
- Batching recovers most of the bandwidth loss: Llama 3.1 8B goes from 20.5 tok/s at BS1 to 368 tok/s at BS32 (LMSYS) and 924.1 tok/s at 128 concurrency (StorageReview); Nemotron 49B goes 5.79 -> 695.1 tok/s at 256 concurrency (120.1x, Dendro Logic).
- Single-stream dense decode is bandwidth-bound and slow: 2.7 tok/s for Llama 3.1 70B FP8, 5.79 tok/s for Nemotron 49B NVFP4, 22.71 tok/s for Qwen3 14B NVFP4.
- DGX Spark bandwidth is 3.7x lower than an RTX 4090 (1,008 GB/s), 6.6x lower than an RTX 5090 (1,792 GB/s), and 12.3x lower than an H100 SXM (~3,350 GB/s).
- H100 SXM/PCIe/NVL have 7 NVDEC and ZERO NVENC; A100 has 5 NVDEC and ZERO NVENC — renting one means no hardware video encoding at all (verified by parsing NVIDIA's support matrix).
- DGX Spark requires CUDA 13.0 and exposes compute capability sm_121; nearly all pip wheels ship cu12x and fail with 'ImportError: libcudart.so.12'.
- flash-attn must not be installed on DGX Spark and FlashInfer has no SM121 support; PyTorch 2.9.0+cu130, vLLM cu130 nightlies, transformers, accelerate, DeepSpeed and Triton do work (natolambert/dgx-spark-setup).
- Reported real-world DGX Spark instability: FP16 GPU inference produces inf/nan (BF16 works), and memory fragmentation during long training causes system-level hard freezes requiring 2-3 hour session caps and empty_cache() every 50 steps.
- On unified memory, GPU OOM equals system OOM — the setup guide recommends disabling swap, a ~100 GB cgroup cap, and OOMScoreAdjust=-1000 on sshd.
- DGX Spark has 4 TB internal NVMe (measured 11.4 GiB/s seq read), 1x 10 GbE RJ-45, and 2x QSFP ConnectX-7 at up to 200 Gb/s; PSU is 240 W with a 140 W GB10 TDP.
- MEASURED on nighthawk 2026-09-13 via /usr/bin/networkQuality: uplink 196.823 Mbps, downlink 83.068 Mbps, idle latency 44.5 ms — so 10 TB takes 112.9 h (4.70 days) at 100% line rate, ~161 h (6.72 days) at 70% goodput.
- RunPod 2026 pricing: Community RTX 4090 $0.34/hr, A100 PCIe $1.19/hr, H100 PCIe $1.99/hr; Lambda H100 SXM $2.99/hr, A100 80GB $2.06/hr; Vast.ai H100 SXM realistically $1.49-2.21/hr.
- A 10 TB RunPod network volume costs ~$532/month (first 1 TB at $0.07/GB, remainder at $0.05/GB); RunPod charges no egress but AWS/GCP charge $0.09-0.12/GB, i.e. $900-1,200 to pull 10 TB back out.
- DGX Spark launched at $3,999 in October 2025; multiple 2026 secondary sources report the Founders Edition MSRP was raised to $4,699 in February 2026 due to LPDDR5X supply constraints.
- tailscale status on 2026-09-13 shows truenas-scale OFFLINE (the likely clip host) while blackbird (Windows) is online — the 10 TB corpus is currently unreachable from this Mac.
- The repo's own frame extractor at ~/Documents/GitHub/valorant-clips/src/valclips/ai/keyframes.py:44-56 spawns one 'ffmpeg -ss {ts} -i {file} -frames:v 1' subprocess per frame — exactly the seek-per-frame pattern NVIDIA's benchmark shows is 11-24x slower than sequential decode.
