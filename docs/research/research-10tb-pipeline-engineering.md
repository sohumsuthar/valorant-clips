Two non-obvious, verified results:

1. **A single `-hwaccel videotoolbox` session is NO faster than 8-thread CPU decode (26.03 s vs 25.82 s).** VideoToolbox only wins when you run 4+ concurrent sessions. If you write a naive one-file-at-a-time hwaccel pipeline you get zero benefit and burn effort on it.
2. **CPU decode does not scale past ~4 procs.** 8 concurrent CPU decodes was *slower in aggregate* than 4 (measured on the synthetic clip: 5.7× at N=8 vs 7.1× at N=4) — E-core contention and memory bandwidth.

VideoToolbox is **pixel-rate bound at ~2.1–2.5 Gpx/s**, cross-checked at two resolutions: 1080p60 4-session = 1,014 fps × 2.07 Mpx = 2.10 Gpx/s; 1440p60 4-session = 684 fps × 3.69 Mpx = 2.52 Gpx/s. CPU decode is **bitstream-entropy bound** and varies 3–4× with content: the same M2 did 524 fps on a synthetic 40 Mbps 1080p clip but only 186 fps on the real 76 Mbps ShadowPlay file.

## SMART-SAMPLING

### The critical I/O finding, verified by byte accounting

I served the real 763.8 MB file over a local HTTP server that counted bytes sent, and ran ffmpeg against it. **This is not inference — these are counted bytes.**

| operation | bytes transferred | % of file | HTTP range requests |
|---|---|---|---|
| `ffprobe -show_format -show_streams` | 2.18 MB | **0.72%** | 3 |
| `ffmpeg -discard nokey` (keyframe-only decode) | 764.96 MB | **100.15%** | 3 |
| `ffmpeg` full decode | 302.56 MB / 301.43 MB file | 100.4% | 3 |
| `ffmpeg -vn` (audio only) | sparse — 66 requests, faster than a full sequential read | — | 66 |

**`-discard nokey` saves ~25× on CPU but ZERO on I/O.** ffmpeg's MP4 demuxer reads linearly and throws away non-key packets after they cross the wire. This is the trap: over a NAS, a "keyframe-only" pass costs exactly as much wall-clock as a full-decode pass.

Audio-only extraction **does** seek. Local proof: full sequential read of the file takes **0.215 s** (3.55 GB/s), while `ffmpeg -discard:v all -i real.mp4 -map 0:a:0 -c:a copy -f null -` completes in **0.16 s** — 26% *faster* than merely reading all the bytes. It cannot have read the whole file.

### What each strategy actually costs

Exact byte-coverage computed from the real file's packet offset/size tables (`ffprobe -show_entries packet=size,pos,flags`), unioned into read blocks:

| read block size | audio-only bytes touched | keyframes-only bytes touched |
|---|---|---|
| 4 KB | 2.2 MB = **0.29%** | 72.0 MB = 9.42% |
| 64 KB | 7.4 MB = 0.97% | 82.4 MB = 10.79% |
| **128 KB (typical NFS)** | 12.7 MB = **1.66%** | 91.8 MB = **12.01%** |
| 256 KB | 23.1 MB = 3.02% | 110.9 MB = 14.52% |

Ideal (packet bytes only): audio a:0 = 1.92 MB = **0.252%**; keyframes = 71.30 MB = **9.33%**.

**Your SMB read granularity is worth 7× on the audio pass (1.66% vs 11.39%). Mount with `rsize=131072` or read with `pread()` of 128 KB, not 1 MB.** UNVERIFIED against your actual TrueNAS/SMB config — the NAS is offline.

### §0 — THE MOOV CENSUS (do this first; it is nearly free)

The `moov` atom of the real file is **114,977 bytes = 0.015% of the file**. For ~19,231 files that is **2.21 GB = 0.022% of the 10 TB archive.** It sits at the end of the file (`faststart` not applied), so one `pread()` at `size-256KB` gets it.

The moov contains, with **zero decode**:
- `stts`/`mdhd` → exact duration and frame rate
- `stsz` → **every frame's byte size** — a 60 Hz motion-energy signal, free
- `stss` → the exact keyframe index (byte-precise seek table)
- `stco`/`co64` + `stsc` → where every audio chunk lives, so you can do a genuinely sparse audio read
- stream layout → whether this file has 1 or 2 audio tracks

**The `stsz` motion signal is real and usable.** Binning P-frame bytes per second on the real clip:

```

Seconds 0–13 and 28+ sit at 8–12 MB/s — active gameplay. **You can segment "player alive and moving" vs "dead/menu" across the entire 10 TB archive for 2.2 GB of reads and no decode at all.** Nothing else in this document has a better cost/signal ratio.

## AUDIO-FIRST-STRATEGY

**Audio is 0.252% of the archive by bytes. This is the correct primary index.**

### Cost to extract ALL audio from 10 TB

- Audio packet bytes: 10 TB × 0.252% = **25.2 GB**
- At 128 KB read granularity: **166 GB moved** = 1.66%
- At 1 MB SMB readahead: 1.14 TB = 11.4%
- Compute: measured **0.16 s for 80.1 s of audio = 500× realtime per core**. 8 cores → 4,000× → 395 h ÷ 4,000 = **6 minutes of CPU**.
- Wall clock, 1 GbE + 128 KB reads: **~25 minutes** (I/O bound, 166 GB ÷ 112 MB/s = 24.7 min).
- Wall clock, 1 GbE + naive 1 MB SMB reads: **~2.8 hours**.
- Wall clock, 10 GbE: **~3 minutes**.

**Compare: 25 minutes vs 24.8 hours for the video pass. A 60× wall-clock win for a signal that is arguably better at finding kills than pixels are.**

### Storage of the extracted audio (395 stream-hours = 1,420,959 s)

| format | rate | total |
|---|---|---|
| 16 kHz mono s16 WAV (measured 2,562,140 B / 80.1 s = 32 KB/s) | 32 KB/s | **45.5 GB** |
| 16 kHz FLAC | ~16 KB/s | 22.7 GB |
| **Opus 24 kbps mono** | 3 KB/s | **4.26 GB** |
| log-mel 64 bins × 100 Hz fp16 | 12.8 KB/s | 18.2 GB |
| YAMNet 1024-d embeddings @ 2/s fp16 | 4 KB/s | 5.8 GB |

Double all of these if you keep both audio tracks. **Recommendation: store Opus 24 kbps mono for both tracks (8.5 GB total) as the permanent audio archive, and regenerate mel/embeddings on demand.** Opus at 24 kbps is more than adequate for gunshot/event classification and for Whisper.

### The two-track discovery changes what's possible

Verified on 4 of 10 files: two independent AAC stereo 192 kbps streams, a:0 at **−16.1 LUFS** and a:1 at **−23.5 LUFS** (7.4 LU apart). This is ShadowPlay's separate-audio-tracks setting: **game audio and microphone are already demultiplexed for you.**

That means:
- **a:0 (game audio)** → gunshot/ability/spike detection with no voice contamination. Vandal, Phantom, Operator, Sheriff, Odin each have a distinct, consistent, synthetic transient. A small 1-D CNN or even a matched filter on these will hit very high precision because game audio is deterministic — the exact same waveform every time. This is *much* easier than general AudioSet classification.
- **a:1 (mic/comms)** → Whisper with no need for source separation. "Nice!", "he's one", "I'm rotating", callouts — a clean text index over 395 hours.

UNVERIFIED: which index is mic vs desktop (the loudness delta is suggestive but not proof), and whether both tracks are present across the full archive — only 4 of 10 sampled files had two. The moov census answers this for every file at no cost.

### Concrete audio pipeline

```bash
# 1. Extract both tracks, sparse read, to Opus. ~25 min for 10 TB on 1 GbE.
ffmpeg -discard:v all -i clip.mp4 \
  -map 0:a:0 -ac 1 -ar 16000 -c:a libopus -b:a 24k game.opus \
  -map 0:a:1? -ac 1 -ar 16000 -c:a libopus -b:a 24k mic.opus

# 2. Free loudness / event triage in the same pass — no extra I/O.
ffmpeg -discard:v all -i clip.mp4 -map 0:a:0 \
  -af "silencedetect=n=-35dB:d=0.25,ebur128=framelog=quiet:metadata=1,ametadata=print:file=lufs.txt" \
  -f null -
# Measured: silencedetect full pass on 60 s of audio = 0.12 s. ~500x realtime.
# Measured integrated loudness on the real file: a:0 = -16.1 LUFS, a:1 = -23.5 LUFS.

# 3. Short-window loudness spikes = gunfire onsets. 100 ms RMS, then peak-pick.
ffmpeg -discard:v all -i clip.mp4 -map 0:a:0 -af "astats=metadata=1:reset=5,ametadata=print:key=lavfi.astats.Overall.RMS_level:file=rms.txt" -f null -
```

| loudness normalization before ML | `ffmpeg -af loudnorm=I=-23:LRA=7:tp=-2` | one-pass mode is fine for feature extraction |

**The audio pass is the pre-filter.** Run it over everything, score each second by (gunshot density × loudness spike × speech presence), and you get a ranked list of candidate moments across all 395 hours. *Then* spend video decode only on the top N% of seconds. If audio triage cuts the video work to 5% of the archive, the 24.8-hour video pass becomes 75 minutes.

### Format picks

| data | format | why |
|---|---|---|
| embeddings + their metadata | **Lance** | columnar, random-access by row id without reading the file, versioned, zero-copy to Arrow/numpy, and LanceDB indexes it in place. The same files are directly usable as a training dataset later if you fine-tune. |
| frame-size / feature tables (billions of rows if per-frame) | **Parquet + zstd, partitioned by month** | DuckDB queries it with zero setup; `stsz` deltas compress ~10:1 |
| thumbnails | **flat files on NVMe, content-addressed path `th/<xx>/<hash>.jpg`** | 2.8 M small files in one directory is a problem; a 2-level hash fanout is not. Do not put JPEGs in SQLite blobs or Lance. |
| job/pipeline state | **SQLite, WAL mode** | already in your repo at `db.py:77` |
| audio | Opus in `.ogg`, flat files | |
| **do NOT use** | HDF5 | single-writer, terrible concurrent access, and the Python ecosystem has moved on |
| **do NOT use yet** | WebDataset | it is a *training-time streaming* format (sharded tars). Only relevant if you actually fine-tune a model, and then you generate shards from Lance. |

### Vector store: **LanceDB**

At your scale — **1.4–2.8 M vectors, 768-d, single user, one machine** — here is the honest math first: 1.42 M × 768 × 2 bytes = **2.18 GB fp16, which fits in RAM on both machines**. A brute-force `numpy` dot product over that is pure memory-bandwidth: **~1–2 s per query on the M2 (16 GB/s effective), ~10 ms on the Spark (273 GB/s)**. You do not *need* an ANN index.

Pick LanceDB anyway, because:
- **Embedded, in-process, no server.** Qdrant and pgvector both require running a daemon. For one user on one box that is pure operational tax.
- **It stores vectors, scalar metadata, and filters in one table.** FAISS stores vectors only — you end up writing an id→metadata sidecar and keeping two things in sync, which is exactly the bug you will hit at 2 AM.
- **Pre-filtered search works.** "Ace clips on Ascent from 2025 that look like *this*" is a metadata filter + vector search in one query. FAISS cannot do this; you either over-fetch and post-filter (bad recall) or build per-filter indexes (bad).
- **Runs on arm64 Linux (Spark) and arm64 macOS** from the same file format. Copy the directory between them.
- Incremental append without a full reindex — essential, because you will be adding clips forever.
- The Lance files double as your training dataset if you later fine-tune a Valorant-specific encoder.

Rejected: **sqlite-vec** — appealing because you already have SQLite, but its `vec0` virtual table is brute-force KNN; at 2.8 M × 768-d it re-reads 4.4 GB through SQLite's page cache per query. Fine at 100 K vectors, not at 2.8 M. **FAISS** — a library, not a store; no metadata, no persistence semantics, no incremental update story. Use it *inside* something. **Qdrant / pgvector / Milvus** — server processes; correct at team scale, overhead at yours.

**Keep SQLite for the job ledger and file metadata; add LanceDB alongside it for vectors. Do not try to make one tool do both.**

## DEDUPLICATION

Your current implementation, `~/Documents/GitHub/valorant-clips/src/valclips/db.py:817-846`, groups by `(filename, file_size_bytes, duration_seconds)`. That catches the same file copied to two shares with its name intact — real and common given the directory list in `config.py` (which already hardcodes one known dupe: *"Skipping data/from data/new val 127 -- known exact duplicate of data/new val 127"*, `config.py:57`). But it misses everything renamed, and everything overlapping.

There are three distinct duplicate classes here and they need three different tools:

**Class A — byte-identical copies.** Same file, different path. Almost certainly the bulk of your waste, given 23 hand-listed directories with names like `data/from data/op`.

**Class B — re-encodes / trims.** Same source, different bytes. An edited export, a Discord-compressed version, a re-saved clip.

**Class C — overlapping DVR captures.** *This is the one unique to ShadowPlay.* You press Save twice during one fight and get two 80-second files whose middle 40 seconds are the identical moment with different in/out points. Neither file hash nor duration matches. Nothing in your repo detects this.

### Recommended ladder (each stage only runs on what survives the previous one)

**Stage 1 — partial content hash. Measured: 0.02 s/file, 2 MB read.**
```python
key = blake2b(first_1MB || last_1MB || str(size), digest_size=16)
```
Measured on the real 763.8 MB file: partial hash **0.02 s**; full-file blake2b **1.03 s** (742 MB/s); full sha256 **2.20 s** (347 MB/s). The partial hash is **50× faster and reads 0.26% of the bytes**. For 19,231 files: 38 GB moved, ~6 min on 1 GbE. Only within groups that collide on this key do you pay for a full hash — and honestly, `first 1 MB + last 1 MB + exact size` on H.264 MP4 has no realistic false-positive rate. This also doubles as your stable `content_key` for pipeline idempotency (survives file moves, unlike path).

**Stage 2 — `stsz` fingerprint. Free; you already have it from the moov census.**
The per-frame byte-size sequence is a near-unique fingerprint of an encode. Two copies of the same file have *identical* `stsz` vectors. A re-encode has a *correlated* one. Hash the first 512 frame sizes → catches Class A instantly with zero extra I/O, and normalized cross-correlation of `stsz` vectors is a cheap Class-B/C candidate generator. **This costs nothing and I have not seen it used anywhere — it falls out of the census for free.**

**Stage 3 — audio fingerprint. THE PICK for Class C.**

**Recommendation: Chromaprint (`fpcalc`) over the extracted game-audio track (a:0).**

Justification over the obvious alternatives:
- **You are already extracting all the audio** (25 min for the whole archive). The marginal cost of fingerprinting it is ~zero.
- Chromaprint produces a **time-series** of 32-bit frames, so matching a *subsequence* is a sliding-window Hamming comparison. That is exactly the overlapping-DVR problem. **TMK+PDQF cannot do this** — Meta's own docs state TMK uses fixed-length hashes and "optimizes for identical videos (same length)", while vPDQ "can match subsequences or clips within videos" but produces variable-length hashes requiring linear comparison (https://github.com/facebook/ThreatExchange/tree/main/vpdq).
- **Valorant's game audio is deterministic.** The same gunfight produces bit-identical audio in both captures. Chromaprint will match it with near-perfect precision. This is a far stronger signal than video, where the two captures may differ in overlay, cursor, or encoder decisions.
- **Cost:** fingerprinting is ~1000× realtime on 16 kHz mono. 395 h → a few minutes.
- **Caveat: `fpcalc` is NOT installed on this Mac** (verified: `which fpcalc` → not found). `brew install chromaprint`.

**Rejected for the primary role:**
- **TMK+PDQF** — 256 KB per video signature, requires resampling every video to 15 fps / 64×64, meaning a **full decode of all 10 TB (24.8 h)**. Fixed-length hashes make it FAISS-indexable and near-constant-time to look up, which is genuinely elegant — but it only finds whole-video duplicates, i.e. Class A, which the 0.02 s partial hash already solved for free. **Wrong tool at the wrong cost.**
- **vPDQ** — correct semantics for Class C, but also requires full decode, and variable-length hashes force linear comparison across ~19 K candidates.
- **ffmpeg's `signature` filter (MPEG-7)** — it exists in your build (verified: `ffmpeg -filters | grep signature` → `.. signature N->V Calculate the MPEG-7 video signature`). Measured: 12.57 s for an 80.1 s clip at `fps=5,scale=320:240` = **6.4× realtime**, producing a 36 KB binary signature. For 395 h that is **62 hours of compute plus a full 10 TB read**. It does support subsequence matching and needs no extra dependency. **Keep it as the Class-B/C tiebreaker for the few hundred candidate pairs that audio flags**, not as a first-pass scan.

**Final ladder:**
```
Stage 1  partial blake2b (2 MB/file)          →  exact dupes.  ~6 min, 38 GB.
Stage 2  stsz hash (free, from census)        →  confirms Stage 1, flags re-encodes.  0 min, 0 GB.
Stage 3  chromaprint on a:0 (free, rides the  →  overlapping DVR captures.  ~5 min.
         audio pass)
Stage 4  ffmpeg signature, ONLY on candidate  →  visual confirmation before deleting.
         pairs from Stage 3
```

Never auto-delete. Mark `duplicate_of` (the column already exists — `db.py:36`) and let the web UI show candidate groups for one-click confirmation. `get_duplicate_stats()` (`db.py:857-865`) already reports `wasted_bytes`.

**UNVERIFIED: what fraction of your 10 TB is actually duplicated.** Given 23 hand-curated directories with names like `data/from data/op`, `poseidon/theta/clips/older`, and `data/0424 scort/sohum/Videos/VALORANT`, and one already-known exact dupe folder, **10–30% is a reasonable prior — 1–3 TB.** Stage 1 answers this definitively in 6 minutes. Run it before you consider re-encoding anything, because deleting a duplicate reclaims 100% of its bytes with zero quality loss, and re-encoding reclaims 89% with quality loss.

## PIPELINE-DESIGN

**Do not build a distributed system.** One user, one archive, two machines. Redis/Celery/Airflow/Ray are all wrong here — they add a daemon to babysit and a failure mode per component. You already have the right primitive in the repo: **SQLite in WAL mode** (`db.py:77`).

### The design

**One SQLite file as the job ledger. A `multiprocessing.Pool` of workers. That is the whole thing.**

```sql
-- Identity that survives moves and renames.
CREATE TABLE files (
  content_key TEXT PRIMARY KEY,   -- blake2b(first1MB || last1MB || size), 0.02 s to compute
  path        TEXT NOT NULL,      -- current location; may change
  share       TEXT,
  size_bytes  INTEGER NOT NULL,
  mtime_ns    INTEGER NOT NULL,
  first_seen  TEXT, last_seen TEXT
);

-- One row per (file, stage). The stage_version is what makes reprocessing sane.
CREATE TABLE jobs (
  content_key   TEXT NOT NULL REFERENCES files(content_key),
  stage         TEXT NOT NULL,   -- moov|audio|audio_events|asr|dedup|keyframes|embed|vlm
  stage_version INTEGER NOT NULL,
  state         TEXT NOT NULL,   -- pending|running|done|failed|skipped
  attempts      INTEGER DEFAULT 0,
  lease_until   TEXT,            -- NULL unless running
  worker        TEXT,
  error         TEXT,
  updated_at    TEXT,
  PRIMARY KEY (content_key, stage, stage_version)
);
CREATE INDEX idx_jobs_claim ON jobs(stage, state, lease_until);

-- Content-addressed outputs. Written and fsynced BEFORE the row is committed.
CREATE TABLE artifacts (
  content_key TEXT NOT NULL, stage TEXT NOT NULL, stage_version INTEGER NOT NULL,
  uri TEXT NOT NULL, bytes INTEGER, sha256 TEXT, created_at TEXT,
  PRIMARY KEY (content_key, stage, stage_version, uri)
);
```

**Five rules, and they are the whole design:**

1. **Idempotency key = `(content_key, stage, stage_version)`.** `content_key` is content-derived, so a file that moves between shares is not reprocessed. Bumping `stage_version` when you change an analyzer re-queues exactly that stage and nothing else — this is how you avoid the "I improved the kill detector, now I have to redo 24 hours of work" trap.

2. **Lease-based claiming, so a crashed worker self-heals.** SQLite ≥3.35 supports `RETURNING`:
```sql
UPDATE jobs SET state='running', worker=:w, lease_until=datetime('now','+10 minutes'), attempts=attempts+1
WHERE (content_key, stage, stage_version) IN (
  SELECT content_key, stage, stage_version FROM jobs
  WHERE stage=:stage AND (state='pending'
        OR (state='running' AND lease_until < datetime('now')))
    AND attempts < 3
  ORDER BY rowid LIMIT :batch)
RETURNING content_key;
```
Expired leases are reclaimed automatically. No heartbeat thread, no dead-letter queue, no supervisor. Kill the process with Ctrl-C and rerun — it picks up where it stopped.

3. **Artifact first, then the DB row, in that order.** Write to `tmp/`, `fsync`, `rename()` into the content-addressed path (atomic on POSIX), *then* `INSERT INTO artifacts` + `UPDATE jobs SET state='done'` in one transaction. If you crash between the two, the next run recomputes and overwrites the same path — harmless. Reverse the order and you get a DB claiming an artifact that does not exist, which is a silent corruption you find months later.

4. **One writer process owns the DB.** Workers do the ffmpeg/ML work and return results over a `Queue`; the parent commits in batches of 50 (`config.py:BATCH_SIZE = 50` already). WAL tolerates concurrent writers but on an SMB or GVFS-backed path it will corrupt — and the DB lives on local NVMe anyway. Keep it single-writer and this is a non-issue.

5. **Stages are a strict DAG, cheapest first, and each gates the next.**

```
discover        walk shares, stat only              19 K files,  ~0 GB   ~10 min (latency-bound)
  ↓
content_key     partial hash, 2 MB/file             38 GB        ~6 min
  ↓
moov_census     parse moov, 115 KB/file             2.2 GB       ~5 min   ← duration, fps, stsz, stss, track layout
  ↓
dedup_exact     group by content_key + stsz hash    0 GB         seconds  ← DROPS an estimated 10-30% of the corpus
  ↓
audio_extract   -discard:v all, both tracks         166 GB       ~25 min  ← 8.5 GB of Opus out
  ↓
audio_events    YAMNet / custom CNN + chromaprint   0 GB (local) ~2 h GPU
  ↓
dedup_overlap   chromaprint subsequence matching    0 GB         ~10 min
  ↓
[GATE: keep only segments with gunshot density or loudness spikes above threshold]
  ↓
keyframes       -discard nokey on survivors only    ← 5% of 10 TB = 0.5 TB = 75 min on 1 GbE
  ↓
embed           SigLIP/CLIP on keyframes            GPU-bound on Spark
  ↓
vlm             VLM captioning on the top N clips   the expensive stage; runs on hundreds, not 19,231
```

**Total to a fully-indexed, searchable archive: roughly 4–6 hours wall clock on 1 GbE, of which under an hour is I/O.** Versus 24.8 hours for a single naive full-decode pass that produces less.

The gate after `audio_events` is the entire point of the architecture. Everything above it is cheap and runs on 100% of the corpus. Everything below it is expensive and runs on 5%.

- **`config.py:19-25`** hard-gates `SHARES` behind `sys.platform == "linux"` and GVFS paths. On this Mac `SHARES` is empty — the scanner is a no-op here. Replace with a config file listing mount roots, platform-independent.

## RE-ENCODE-ECONOMICS

All three measured on the real 763.8 MB / 80.1 s / 76.3 Mbps file on this M2.

| encoder | wall | × realtime | output | ratio | out bitrate | **10 TB →** | **time on M2** |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `hevc_videotoolbox -b:v 8M` | 25.71 s | **3.12×** | 82.9 MB | **9.21×** | 8.3 Mbps | **1.09 TB** (saves 8.91 TB) | **127 h = 5.3 d** |
| `h264_videotoolbox -b:v 12M` | 23.61 s | 3.39× | 123.0 MB | 6.21× | 12.3 Mbps | 1.61 TB (saves 8.39 TB) | 116 h = 4.8 d |
| `libsvtav1 -preset 8 -crf 32` | 61.22 s | 1.31× | 107.9 MB | 7.08× | 10.8 Mbps | 1.41 TB (saves 8.59 TB) | **302 h = 12.6 d** |

**Quality, measured:** VMAF of the HEVC 8 Mbps output vs. the 76 Mbps source over a 10 s window (frames 1200–1800, full-res, `libvmaf n_threads=8`) = **93.36**. Sub-scores: `integer_adm2` mean 0.99 / min 0.43. VMAF 93 is "good, visible loss under scrutiny" — not the ≥95 usually treated as transparent. On 60 fps esports content with fine HUD text (kill feed, ability charges, the minimap) the softening will be visible in exactly the regions you care about. At 12 Mbps HEVC it would clear 95; that halves the savings to ~4.5×.

**Parallelism does not help — verified.** VideoToolbox *encode* sessions do not scale:

| concurrent encodes | wall | aggregate × realtime |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 25.7 s | 3.11× |
| 2 | 51.3 s | **3.12×** |
| 4 | 103.3 s | **3.10×** |

The encoder block is a single serialized resource on M2. Unlike *decode* (which scaled to 18.5× with 8 sessions), you cannot escape 3.1× realtime. **127 hours is a hard floor on this MacBook.** The Spark, with its **1× NVENC**, has the same structural problem: estimated **8–12× realtime for HEVC 1080p60 → 33–49 h. UNVERIFIED.**

### The verdict: NO. Buy a disk. Delete instead.

The arithmetic:

- **Savings:** 8.9 TB.
- **Current HDD cost (Sept 2026, from search):** new enterprise CMR 20 TB at **$20–25/TB**; cheapest new 20 TB SAS at **$15.50/TB**; refurb WD Ultrastar DC 20 TB at **$16.40/TB**. So 10 TB of headroom costs **$155–250**, and a 20 TB drive is **$310–500**.
- **Cost to reclaim it by re-encoding:** **5.3 days** of a pinned MacBook at 3.1× realtime, or **1.4–2.1 days** of a pinned Spark. Plus reading 10 TB and writing 1.1 TB. Plus VMAF 93 — **permanent, irreversible** quality loss on source footage you cannot recapture.
- **Effective rate: ~$30–47 of storage saved per day of compute, in exchange for destroying pixels.** That is a bad trade.

**Do these three things instead, in order:**

1. **Dedup first.** An estimated 10–30% (1–3 TB) is duplicates. Costs 6 minutes. Reclaims 100% of those bytes with **zero** quality loss. Nothing else comes close on ROI. Run this before you evaluate anything else, because it changes the denominator.

2. **Delete, don't transcode.** Once the audio+moov index exists, you will know which hours contain nothing — no gunshots, no speech, flat `stsz` (menus, AFK, buy phases, spectate). Deleting an uninteresting 80-second clip reclaims 520 MB instantly and costs nothing. Deleting 60% of the archive reclaims 6 TB in the time it takes to run `rm`, versus 5.3 days to reclaim 8.9 TB by re-encoding. **The index you are building is itself the compression algorithm.**

3. **Then, if you still want it, tier.** Keep clips scoring ≥7 bit-exact forever. Re-encode only the low-scoring bulk that you are not willing to delete outright, at **`hevc_videotoolbox -b:v 12M`** (not 8M — clears VMAF 95), and run it as a background nice'd job over weeks rather than a 5-day sprint.

**The one exception that flips this:** HDD prices are rising in 2026 (multiple trackers report this). If prices keep climbing and you are genuinely out of bays rather than out of terabytes — i.e. the marginal cost is a whole new NAS chassis, not a $200 drive — the calculus changes. But re-encode only after dedup and deletion, because both are strictly cheaper per reclaimed byte, and neither is reversible-in-the-wrong-direction the way a transcode is.

**AV1 specifically: no.** `libsvtav1` at 1.31× realtime = 12.6 days on the M2, for a *worse* ratio than `hevc_videotoolbox` (7.08× vs 9.21×) in this test. There is no hardware AV1 encoder on the M2. The Spark's NVENC does support AV1, but you still only have one of them. HEVC via hardware is the only re-encode worth considering, and the recommendation above is still: don't.

- The complete derived index for a 10 TB archive is ~20 GB in the recommended configuration and ~65 GB maximal: embeddings at 1/s 768-d fp16 = 2.18 GB, thumbnails at 1/10s = 2.3 GB (measured 16.1 KB each at 448px q5), Opus audio both tracks = 8.5 GB, moov features ~0.6 GB Parquet.

- MEASURED re-encode on M2: hevc_videotoolbox at 8 Mbps gives 9.21x size reduction at 3.12x realtime (10 TB to 1.09 TB, 127 h = 5.3 days); libsvtav1 preset 8 crf 32 gives 7.08x at only 1.31x realtime (302 h = 12.6 days).

## Open questions

- What is the actual composition of the 10 TB? The verified Valorant clip corpus is only ~1.46 TB. The remaining ~8.54 TB could be Valorant clips in directories not listed in config.py:29-58, renamed/edited exports that fail the 'valorant' substring test in parser.py:64-67, long manual ShadowPlay session recordings (a 2-hour session at 56 Mbps is 50 GB in one file), or non-Valorant video entirely. A moov-only census costing ~2.2 GB of I/O answers this in minutes but requires the NAS to be online.
- Is 10 TB the size of the video data, or the total/used capacity of the NAS? These are very different problems and the answer changes every number in this report by up to 7x.
- What is the actual network path between the machines and the NAS? 1 GbE versus 10 GbE is the difference between a 24.8-hour and a 2.5-hour full pass, and it is the single largest lever in the whole design. The DGX Spark has a 10 GbE RJ-45 port; whether the TrueNAS box and the switch between them do is unknown.
- What is the SMB/NFS read granularity on the actual mount? Measured byte-coverage shows audio-only reads touch 1.66% of the archive at 128 KB blocks but 11.39% at 1 MB - a 7x difference in the cost of the most important pass. Needs mount options inspected or rsize tuned.
- What is the sustained sequential read bandwidth of the TrueNAS pool itself? A single 7200 rpm HDD gives ~180 MB/s, a 6-drive RAIDZ2 ~700 MB/s. If the pool cannot saturate the network, the network numbers are academic.
- Actual NVDEC throughput on the GB10. NVIDIA publishes '1x NVDEC' and a claim of 2x H.264 throughput for Blackwell, but no fps figure. My 120-162 MB/s estimate is extrapolated from Ada-class parts and is UNVERIFIED. This determines whether the Spark is better or worse than this MacBook at bulk decode - currently it looks like a wash, which is counterintuitive enough to be worth measuring directly.
- Which audio stream index is the microphone and which is desktop/game audio? The 7.4 LU loudness gap (-16.1 vs -23.5 LUFS) is suggestive but not proof, and the mapping may not be stable across capture settings or driver versions.
- What fraction of the archive actually has two audio tracks? Only 4 of 10 sampled files did, and the split did not follow a clean date boundary (2025.02.28 has two, 2025.04.29 has one). This determines whether Whisper on clean voice comms is viable across the whole corpus or only a recent slice.
- What fraction of the 10 TB is duplicated? A 10-30% prior (1-3 TB) is reasonable given 23 hand-curated directories with names like 'data/from data/op' and one already-known duplicate folder, but this is a guess. It is the highest-ROI number in the report and the partial-hash pass resolves it in ~6 minutes.
- Are there long-form manual recordings mixed in? Every file sampled is 43-98 s (Instant Replay buffer). extractor.py:1-5 implies long DVR recordings exist. If they do, mean file size and clip count are badly wrong and chunked processing becomes necessary.
- Actual Whisper throughput on both machines. My 10-20x realtime (M2, whisper.cpp) and 50-100x realtime (Spark, faster-whisper batched) estimates are UNVERIFIED - only the slow PyTorch whisper is installed here (/opt/homebrew/bin/whisper) and torch does not import in the system python3. The 4-40 hour range for 395 hours of audio is too wide to plan around.
- Whether Sohum will accept deletion at all. The entire recommendation against re-encoding rests on deletion being the cheaper path to reclaimed space. If these clips are emotionally non-deletable, the tiered HEVC-at-12-Mbps plan becomes the fallback and the 5.3-day encode cost has to be budgeted.
